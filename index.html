Part 1

The 6 warning signs of a phishing email

Most phishing emails give themselves away. Open each one to learn what to look for — with a real-world example.

Attackers create panic so you act before you think — warnings that your account will be closed, a payment failed, or you'll face a penalty.
⚠ “Your account will be suspended in 24 hours unless you verify immediately.”

The display name looks legit, but the actual domain is subtly wrong — a swapped letter, extra word, or public email service.
From: IT Support <support@micros0ft-helpdesk.com> ← zero instead of “o”

The visible text and the real destination don't match. Hover (or long-press) to preview the true URL before clicking anything.
Text says: office365.com → real link: http://0ffice-verify.ru/login

Mass phishing can't use your name. Real companies you deal with usually address you personally and reference specific details.
“Dear Customer,” / “Dear User,” instead of your actual name

Attachments you weren't expecting — invoices, scans, “secure documents” — can carry malware or open fake login pages.
📎 Invoice_scan.html · Payment_Details.zip

Legitimate IT and banks will never email you to “confirm your password,” MFA code, or full card number. That request is the attack.
“Reply with your username and password to keep your access active.”

Phishing isn't only email

📱 Smishing (SMS)
Fake delivery, bank or toll texts with a short link. Don't tap — open the real app or site yourself.
📞 Vishing (voice)
A caller “from IT or your bank” creates urgency. Hang up and call back on a number you already trust.
🔳 QR phishing
A QR code hides its link. Treat unexpected codes in emails, posters or parking meters like unknown links.
🤖 AI & deepfakes
AI writes flawless emails and can clone a voice or face. Typos are no longer a reliable clue — verify the request, not the polish.

    Why multi-factor authentication matters

    Even if an attacker steals your password, the right second factor stops them. Click each option to see what happens when a thief tries your stolen password.

    🔑

    Password only

    One secret protects everything.

    ❌ Breached — the stolen password is all they needed.
    📲

    Password + SMS / push

    A code or “approve” prompt on your phone.

    ⚠️ Risky — codes can be phished and push prompts spammed until you tap “approve.”
    🔐

    Passkey / security key

    Phishing-resistant (FIDO2).

    ✅ Blocked — the key only works on the real site, so the attacker is stuck.

    Turn on MFA everywhere it's offered. Prefer an authenticator app or a passkey over SMS — and never approve a prompt you didn't start.

    Part 5

    Think you've been phished? Report it.

    Reporting fast limits the damage — for you and everyone else. You will never be blamed for reporting, even if you clicked.

    1
    Don't interact further.
    Don't click, reply, open attachments, or enter anything. Leave the email as-is.
    2
    Report it.
    Use the “Report Phish” button in your mail app, or forward to your IT / security team.
    3
    If you already clicked, say so.
    Tell IT immediately and change your password from a device you trust. Speed beats embarrassment.

    One-click reporting

    Most organizations add a “Report Phishing” button to Outlook/Gmail. Know where yours is before you need it.

    Where to report (outside your workplace)

    • At work: use your mail app's “Report Phish” button or forward to your IT / security team.
    • Anti-Phishing Working Group: forward phishing emails to reportphishing@apwg.org.
    • US Federal Trade Commission: reportfraud.ftc.gov. Forward spam texts to 7726 (SPAM).
    • CISA: cisa.gov/report for incidents at organizations.

    The 30-second takeaway

    Stop. Look. Report. Slow down on anything urgent, check the sender and links, never hand over passwords or MFA codes, turn on strong MFA, and report anything suspicious. When in doubt, verify through a channel you trust — not the email.
